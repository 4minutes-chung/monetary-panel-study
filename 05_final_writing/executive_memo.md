# The Quantity Theory in the Age of QE and COVID
### A 160-Country, 34-Year Revisit of Lucas (1980)

---

## The Question

Print money → get inflation. Is that still true?

The textbook says yes. But central banks printed trillions from 2008 to 2020 and got almost nothing. Then 2021 hit and inflation went to 8%. This project uses 160 countries from 1991 to 2024 to answer: **when does money printing actually cause inflation, and when doesn't it?**

---

## Act 1 — The Long Run: Lucas is Still Right

Lucas (1980) showed a simple fact: countries that grow their money supply faster get more inflation. One-for-one over long horizons. McCandless & Weber (1995) confirmed it. De Grauwe & Polan (2005) confirmed it again.

**We confirm it again, with data through 2024:**

| Sample | Countries | M2 → Inflation slope | R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| All countries (incl. 1990s hyperinflation) | 108 | **0.95** | 0.88 |
| Modern economies only (no hyperinflation) | 83 | **0.52** | 0.53 |

The long-run relationship is alive. In the average modern economy, 1 percentage point more money growth is associated with 0.52 pp more inflation over the long run. Not one-for-one — but real, strong, and significant.

The textbook is not broken. It just operates slowly.

---

## Act 2 — The Short Run: The QE Puzzle Explained

Now look year to year, within each country.

| Sample | M2 → Inflation (year-on-year) | Significant? |
|---|---|---|
| All 160 countries | **0.665** | Yes |
| 123 modern economies | **0.040** | **No** |

**0.04. Near zero.** For the 123 countries that have not had hyperinflation — every advanced economy, most of the developing world — year-to-year money growth has essentially no detectable effect on inflation.

This is the QE decade in one number. The Fed printed $4 trillion from 2008 to 2019. M2 grew 80%. Inflation stayed near 2%. This data shows exactly why: the short-run transmission from money to prices is essentially broken in modern, credible monetary regimes.

The full-sample coefficient of 0.665 is not a contradiction — it comes almost entirely from the post-Soviet and Latin American hyperinflation episodes of the early 1990s (Ukraine 388%, Brazil 308%, Belarus 996%). Those are confirming the textbook at extremes. They are not the modern story.

---

## Act 3 — COVID and Ukraine: A Partial Monetarist Vindication

In 2021–2023, inflation exploded. US: 8%. UK: 11%. Turkey: 80%. The standard monetarist story: governments printed money, now you're paying for it.

We split this question into three cuts.

**Cut 1 — Robustness: COVID doesn't restore the within-country link.**

| | With COVID years | Without COVID years |
|---|---|---|
| Full sample coefficient | 0.665 | 0.664 |
| Modern economies | 0.040 | 0.040 |

Removing 2020–2021 from the data entirely changes nothing. Inclusion or exclusion: same result.

**Cut 2 — Sub-period TWFE: the QE transmission was already dead.**

| Era | M2 → Inflation (within country, year-on-year) | Significant? |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-QE 1991–2007 | **0.113** | Yes |
| QE era 2008–2019 | **0.0005** | No |
| COVID/post-COVID 2020–2024 | **−0.014** | No |

The short-run link was real before 2008. QE killed it. COVID didn't restore it. The within-country, year-on-year monetary transmission has been near-zero since the GFC.

**Cut 3 — Cross-country scatter: between countries, money still mattered.**

This is where the moderate monetarist gets vindication. Across 102 countries: countries that expanded M2 more in 2020–2021 got more inflation in 2021–2023.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Slope (M2 growth 2020–21 → inflation 2021–23) | **0.494** |
| p-value | < 0.0001 |
| R² | 0.255 |
| Countries | 102 |

**The story is cross-sectional, not temporal.** The US printed more than Japan → the US got more inflation than Japan. That's the money story. But within any single country, the year-to-year timing of M2 changes didn't predict when inflation arrived.

**The synthesis:** COVID fiscal stimulus (cash to households, not bank reserves) created a cross-country wedge in inflation outcomes. The QE-era reserves-to-banks transmission remained broken. Both things are true simultaneously.

---

## Act 4 — Inflation Targeting: Institutions Kill the Link

Countries that formally adopted inflation targeting — where the central bank explicitly commits to a number — show even weaker short-run pass-through after adoption. Post-adoption slope shift: **−0.49**.

The intuition: if everyone believes the central bank will hit its target, money growth stops mattering for near-term inflation expectations. **Credibility severs the short-run link.**

This is a pattern, not a causal claim. IT adoption is endogenous — countries adopt it after high-inflation episodes. But the direction is consistent with the broader story.

---

## Act 5 — The US: 60 Years of Evidence

Using US data from 1960 to 2024 with a 5-year moving average (same method as Lucas 1980):

| Relationship | Slope | Note |
|---|---|---|
| M2 growth → Inflation | **0.47** | Borderline significant |
| M2 growth → T-bill rate | **0.42** | Directional only |

Same story. At low frequency, the monetary chain exists. The Fisher relation — more money → more inflation → higher nominal rates — shows up in the data direction, though weakly.

The QE decade (2009–2020) pulls both slopes below the long-run benchmark. Post-2021, the COVID inflation cluster pulls them back. The US is one data point telling the same story as the cross-country panel.

## The Juice — Four Things to Remember

**1. Long run: quantity theory lives.**
Across countries, over decades, money and inflation move together at a 0.5–0.95 slope. Lucas was right.

**2. Short run in modern economies: it disappears.**
Year to year, for the 123 countries that avoid hyperinflation, the slope is 0.04 and insignificant. This has been true since 2008, not just during COVID. The QE decade is not an anomaly — it is the norm.

**3. COVID inflation: monetarist vindication — but only cross-sectionally.**
Within any country, year-to-year M2 changes didn't predict when inflation arrived. But across countries, those that printed more in 2020–2021 got more inflation in 2021–2023 (slope=0.494, R²=0.26). The channel was fiscal transfers to households, not QE reserves to banks.

**4. The distributed lag exists but fades fast.**
M2 growth predicts inflation one year forward (coef=0.101, p=0.006). The two-year lag is zero. Money matters at a 1-year horizon — but the effect is modest and dissipates.

---

## What This Study Cannot Claim

- Causal: we cannot say QE *caused* low inflation or that money printing *prevented* COVID inflation
- Structural: we cannot rule out that the short-run slope would change under a different policy regime
- Precise: the IV identification is too weak for causal estimates

**Claim tier: Obj A and D are descriptive. Obj B and C are exploratory/associational.**

---

## One Paragraph

In long-run country averages, money growth and inflation move together — slope 0.52 to 0.95 depending on sample, consistent with Lucas (1980) and the canonical QTM papers. Year to year within countries, the pass-through collapses to near zero in the 123 non-hyperinflationary economies that define the modern world. Sub-period TWFE confirms the break happened at the GFC: pre-2008 slope was 0.113 (significant), post-2008 it has been near zero in every era including COVID. But the 2021–2023 inflation is not fully unexplained by money: across 102 countries, those that expanded M2 most in 2020–2021 saw the most inflation in 2021–2023 (slope=0.494, R²=0.26). The transmission was cross-sectional — fiscal transfers to households, not reserves to banks — and it operated through a 1-year lag (m2_l1 coef=0.101, p=0.006). The long-run quantity theory survives intact. The short-run within-country version largely does not. But the COVID era offers the moderate monetarist a partial vindication: the mechanism wasn't dead, it just requires the right kind of money, distributed the right way.
